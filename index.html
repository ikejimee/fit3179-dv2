Australia's Great Outdoors

Which adventure activities Australians do, who does them, and where in the country they happen most.

Bethany Aung · Student ID 34960627 · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2

Nearly one in six Australian adults went bush walking in 2025, and participation in outdoor pursuits keeps climbing. But the national average hides sharp differences. A Tasmanian gets outdoors far more than a South Australian, men and women pick different activities, and a sport popular with teenagers can empty out by middle age. This page follows adventure participation across state, sex and age, using the Australian Sports Commission's 2025 AusPlay survey.

Every figure is a share of the adult population who did the activity at least once in 2025. Hover any chart for exact values.

What Australians do outdoors

Start with the simplest question. Which adventure activities draw the most people?

Adventure in Australia is really two stories. A few easy, low-cost activities pull in large crowds, while the specialist pursuits stay small but devoted. The ranking sets that split out from top to bottom.

Key insights

  • Bush walking (17.5%) and cycling (8.3%) together dwarf every other adventure activity.
  • Specialist climbing sits near the bottom. Rock climbing reaches just 0.5% of adults.
  • Low participation does not mean low value. The niche sports still anchor a real tourism and gear economy.

What we see

Bush walking reaches 17.5% of adults and cycling 8.3%. Together they dwarf the field. Below those two, every activity sits under 3%, and specialist climbing barely registers at half a percent.

Why this is the case

The two leaders ask almost nothing of a participant. No gear, no club, no booking, and a trailhead or bike path within reach of most towns. The niche sports each demand equipment, training, or specific terrain, and every one of those hurdles trims the number of people who ever start.

Grouped into families, the same activities show how lopsided adventure really is. Tile size is the participation rate.

Adventure sorts into three families: things you do on land, on water, and on rock. One family towers over the other two.

Key insights

  • Land and trail activities fill most of the space, carried by bush walking and cycling.
  • Water is a distinct but far smaller family, led by surfing and fishing.
  • Climbing is the smallest family, a committed niche.

What we see

Land and trail activities fill most of the tile area, driven almost entirely by bush walking and cycling. Water forms a smaller block led by surfing and fishing, and climbing a thin sliver.

Why this is the case

The land family contains the two no-barrier giants, so it dwarfs the rest. Water and climbing depend on coastline, equipment, or cliffs, and each of those requirements caps how many people ever take part.

Who gets outdoors

The national averages split sharply once you look at who takes part, by sex and by age.

Each line links men and women for one activity. The longer the line, the wider the gap.

Men and women do not pick the same activities. The gap runs widest in the sports that lean on equipment or distance, and it narrows or flips for the ones built around walking and water.

Key insights

  • Men lead cycling by 4.2%, the widest gap, and also lead fishing and mountain biking by wide margins.
  • Women lead only one activity: bush walking, at 18.6% against 16.3%.
  • Climbing and kayaking draw men and women in near-equal numbers.

What we see

Men lead most activities. The gap runs widest in cycling at 4.2 points, with fishing and mountain biking close behind. Women lead a single activity, bush walking, at 18.6% against 16.3%.

Why this is the case

The male-led sports cluster around equipment, speed, and distance, which mirrors long-running participation patterns in those pursuits. Bush walking is the most accessible and social option on the list, and it is the one where women come out in front.

Each panel keeps its own scale so you can read the shape of every activity, not just the biggest.

Age reshapes the outdoors. Some activities hold a steady crowd across the decades, while others belong almost entirely to the young or drift older as the intensity drops. Each panel below keeps its own scale, so read the shape of the curve, not the height against its neighbours.

Key insights

  • Bush walking holds strong from 25 to 64, peaking at 35–44 (21%).
  • Rock climbing is a young person's sport. It tops out at 15–17 and falls away fast.
  • Kayaking runs the other way, climbing to a 55–64 peak as paddlers trade intensity for the water.

What we see

Each activity peaks at a different age. Bush walking holds a broad plateau from 25 to 64. Rock climbing tops out at 15 to 17 and falls away fast. Kayaking rises late to a 55 to 64 peak.

Why this is the case

Physical intensity and appetite for risk fall with age, so the demanding sports shed older participants. The gentler activities hold or gain them, because they offer the outdoors without the strain, which is why walking and paddling skew older.

Which states get outdoors most

Participation is uneven across the country. Some states live outdoors; others lag well behind.

Each state is shaded by how many adults took part, and labelled with that figure: the percentage of adults who did the activity at least once in 2025. Pick an activity to change the map.

Tasmania (27.5%) and the ACT (26.8%) lead the country for bush walking, far above the states with big cities.
South Australia (15.4%) sits lowest, a 12-point gap behind Tasmania.
Switch to cycling and the ACT jumps to the front at 14%. Switch to surfing and the coastal states take over.

What we see

For bush walking, Tasmania (27.5%) and the ACT (26.8%) lead by a clear margin, while South Australia trails at 15.4%. Changing the activity in the dropdown reshuffles the order completely.

Why this is the case

Proximity drives participation. Tasmanians and Canberrans live minutes from bush and trail, so the barrier to a walk is low. The big-city states put more distance and traffic between people and the outdoors, which shows up as lower rates.

Read down a column for a state's outdoor profile. Read across a row to see where one activity concentrates.

The top two rows glow green everywhere. Bush walking and cycling carry the outdoor economy in every state.
Tasmania runs hot across the board, not just bush walking.
Warm-water activities tilt north and west. Fishing peaks in the NT and WA, surfing along the eastern seaboard.

What we see

The bush walking and cycling rows glow in every column, so they lead in all eight states. Tasmania runs warm across the whole grid. Fishing and surfing light up in only a few states.

Why this is the case

The two mainstream activities travel everywhere because they need no special setting. The rest follow climate and coastline. Warm northern waters favour fishing in the NT and WA, and the surf breaks of the eastern seaboard put surfing there.

Australia's national parks and protected land

National parks and reserves are the backdrop for adventure tourism. These two maps show how much land each state protects, and where the parks actually sit.

Circle size shows the total protected area in each state. The number is the share of that state set aside as protected land.

Western Australia protects the most land by far: 78.7 million hectares, almost a third of the state.
By share, Tasmania leads at 42.8%, which fits its chart-topping bush walking.
The populous east protects the least proportionally. NSW sits at 10.4% and Queensland at 9.4%.

What we see

Western Australia holds by far the most protected land, 78.7 million hectares. Measured as a share of the state, Tasmania leads at 42.8%, while New South Wales and Queensland sit around 10%.

Why this is the case

The arid western and central states have vast, lightly settled country that is easy to set aside. The populous east carries farming, housing, and industry competing for the same ground, so a smaller slice ends up protected even where the total hectares are large.

Each dot is one national park, placed at its real location. Read the clusters to see where protected adventure country concentrates.

Parks cluster along the eastern seaboard, the south-west, and across Tasmania, the same places people get outdoors most.
The arid interior holds fewer parks, but each one is far larger.
All 1,413 national parks are shown. Hover any dot for its name, state, and area.

What we see

National parks crowd the eastern seaboard, the south-west corner, and Tasmania, and thin out sharply across the dry interior, where the few parks that exist are very large.

Why this is the case

Parks track both nature and people. The wetter, more varied coasts hold more distinct ecosystems worth protecting and far more visitors to use them. The interior supports fewer species-rich sites, so protection there comes as a handful of enormous desert and savanna reserves.

How outdoor tourism is changing, and where visitors go

Participation tells you who gets outdoors at home. Visitor numbers add a second lens: first, how domestic overnight travel shifted across the states from 2023 to 2024; then, where within each state those nights are actually spent.

Every state starts at 100, its own 2023 level. A line ending above 100 means that state gained visitor nights in 2024; a line ending below 100 means it lost them. The steeper the slope, the bigger the change. Green rose, blue fell.

Australia recorded 402 million visitor nights in 2023, easing to 397 million in 2024. Most of the country gave back some nights, but not everywhere. Each state's 2024 total is labelled on its line.

Key insights

  • Most states lost ground. NSW, Victoria, WA, Tasmania and the ACT all declined.
  • Queensland, South Australia and the Northern Territory bucked the trend and grew.
  • The ACT fell most steeply, from 9.3 to 7.5 million nights.

What we see

Five of the eight states and territories lost visitor nights between 2023 and 2024. Queensland, South Australia and the Northern Territory rose against the trend. The ACT drops the most sharply.

Why this is the case

2023 carried a strong post-pandemic travel rebound that cooled through 2024. The places that kept growing lean on nature and regional touring, which held up, while city-heavy destinations such as the ACT gave back the most.

The same change as a ranking. Distance from zero is the size of the gain or loss.

Ranked by growth, the states separate into a small group that gained and a larger group that slipped, with one clear outlier.

Key insights

  • The Northern Territory grew most, up 6.4%.
  • The ACT fell hardest, down 18.6%, far below any other.
  • Every other state moved only a few points either way.

What we see

The Northern Territory leads at +6.4%, with Queensland and South Australia just above zero. The rest are negative, and the ACT stands apart as a clear outlier at −18.6%.

Why this is the case

Nature and outback destinations like the Northern Territory drew more overnight visitors, which fits the outdoor-travel story of this page. The ACT depends far more on events, business and city travel, so it lost the most as that demand softened.

Capital city nights sit on the left, regional nights on the right. The longer the green bar, the more a state's travel happens out in the country.

Overnight travel is where adventure tourism lives, and in most states it happens well beyond the capital city.

Key insights

  • In NSW, Queensland, Victoria and WA, regional nights far outweigh the capital.
  • The ACT is the mirror image: all of its nights are in Canberra.
  • The Northern Territory splits almost evenly between Darwin and the regions.

What we see

For most states the green regional bar is far longer than the blue capital bar. New South Wales, Queensland, Victoria and WA are strongly regional. The ACT is entirely capital, and the Northern Territory is split roughly in half.

Why this is the case

Regional and outback areas hold the national parks, coast and wilderness that overnight visitors travel for, so the outdoor-heavy states skew regional. The ACT is a single city with little land outside it, so its visitors stay in Canberra.
